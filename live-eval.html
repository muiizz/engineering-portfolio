Applied AI, evaluation

Three models, one contract, three languages

I sent the same 120 support messages to three Claude models, three times each, and scored every reply against a fixed contract. The largest model was the only one to pass everything, at about thirty times the cost of the smallest.

First run: Haiku 5.5 passed 116 of 120, Sonnet 5.5 passed 118, Opus 5.5 passed 120. All routed every urgent message correctly.

The contract

A fictional travel support desk has three routes: reply, hand off to a person, or send to the urgent queue. A reply passes only if the route is right, it contains the required wording for that case, and it avoids two banned phrases. The scorer is the same deterministic code as the offline gate.

What the failures were

No model sent a message to the wrong queue. Every failure was wording. Haiku wrote the desk hours in its own words and translated “support team” into Urdu script, where the contract wants both verbatim. Sonnet failed for a different reason: it told customers it could not promise a guaranteed refund, and the scorer bans that phrase wherever it appears.

That second one is a fault in my scorer. A phrase ban cannot tell a promise from a refusal. It is the same weakness the routing audit found in its rules, and here it cost a correct answer its pass.

Fixing the scorer

I replaced the phrase ban with a check that looks for a negator close to the phrase, then rescored the recorded first-run replies without calling a model. Haiku stayed at 116 and Opus at 120. Sonnet moved from 118 to 120: both of its failures were correct refusals. The new check is still a heuristic, because a reply can negate one thing and promise another.

What broke on the first run

The first run capped replies at 200 output tokens. Urdu script takes several tokens per word, so Urdu replies were cut off mid-sentence, failed to parse, and were scored as failures. That run reported Haiku at 68 of 120 with only 8 of 30 urgent cases passing. The models were fine; my harness was wrong. I raised the cap to 1,000, added a count of truncated and empty replies to every run, and ran it again. The second run recorded none of either.

Retrieval with a learned encoder

The knowledge base lab used hand-written four-number vectors. I replaced them with an open multilingual sentence encoder and scored 36 queries, three per article in each language.

The encoder is weakest on Roman Urdu, which is the script it has seen least. Keywords rescue three of its misses, which is the case for keeping both.

What this does not show

The 120 messages reuse four intents with ten context variants, so they are easier than real traffic. I wrote both the policy prompt and the cases. One provider was tested, with three runs per model, on one day. The 36 retrieval queries were written for this lab. Prices change, so the cost column is a snapshot.
